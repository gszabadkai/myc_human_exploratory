# **STYLE.md — authorial style reference (GS)**

**Purpose.** Constrain drafting and editing of manuscript prose to the author's own voice. Read this before writing or rewriting any Abstract, Introduction, introductory paragraph or Discussion text. It governs _how_ sentences are built, not _what_ they claim; scientific content rules live elsewhere (see §10).

**Status.** v0.1, 2026-09-17. First pass, derived from three papers. Vocabulary and lexical preference are **not yet covered** (§9). Expect revision.

## **3. The four structural moves**

These are the load-bearing rules. Getting the metrics right while getting these wrong still produces text that does not read as the author's.

### **3.1 Background sentence**

Opens on the subject of study, usually with in-line apposition rather than a separate defining sentence: term, abbreviation in parentheses, then a comma and a defining phrase.

> "Mutations of the type 1 ryanodine receptor (RyR1), a key sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca²⁺ release channel almost exclusively expressed in skeletal muscle, are one of the most common causes of non-dystrophic congenital myopathies."

### **3.2 The gap — long noun phrase, flat verdict verb**

The gap is always named explicitly, as a subject noun phrase followed by an unadorned verdict. Never a rhetorical question; never merely implied.

Verdict verbs in use: _is unclear · are unknown · is not known · remains unanswered · are insufficient · is largely unknown_

> "the functional relevance and the relationship of IP3R mediated Ca²⁺ signalling with the pathophysiology of the disease **is unclear**"

> "the full spectrum, characteristics and regulation of existing metabolic switches **are unknown**"

> "How these heterogeneous mutations lead to this range, development and interconversion of pathologies **is not known**"

### **3.3 The pivot — materials and purpose, never the claim**

"Here we ..." takes what was used and what it was used _for_. The finding arrives in the **next** sentence, under "We show that ...".

> "**Here we used** muscle biopsies of central core and multi-minicore disease patients ... **to characterise** global cellular and mitochondrial Ca²⁺ signalling, mitochondrial function and gene expression associated with the disease."

> "**Here we have tested the hypothesis that** IP₃R mediated mitochondrial Ca²⁺ signalling ... underlie the functional phenotype of core myopathies."

Pivot verbs: _used · describe · have tested · aimed to identify_. Do **not** use _show, reveal, demonstrate_ in the pivot — they belong to the sentence after it.

### **3.4 The close**

Either a generalising verdict or a statement of applicability.

> "**Altogether, these data indicate that** remodelling of skeletal muscle Ca²⁺ signalling following loss of functional RyR1 mediates bioenergetic adaptation."

> "The method **is applicable to** any large and heterogeneous transcriptome dataset to discover metabolic and associated pathophysiological states."

### **3.5 Paragraph-terminal sentences carry the argument forward**

Each Introduction paragraph ends on a sentence that states the next step, not on a summary of the one just made.

> "Accordingly, a central question of the pathophysiology of RyR1 related myopathies is the identification of pathways linking RyR1 mutations to mitochondrial dysfunction and loss."

> "To test this hypothesis, here we aimed to identify differential regulation patterns in the full metabolic transcriptome and link these quantitatively with the large scale GEPs associated with different cancer cell states."

## **4. Punctuation and orthography**

- **No em-dashes.** Zero in ~3,500 words of corpus. Use commas, parenthetical apposition, or a subordinate clause.
- **`(i)` / `(ii)` / `(iii)` enumeration** inside a sentence is a signature device and appears in all three papers. Use it where a sentence carries alternatives.
- **Colons** only to introduce an enumeration, never for emphasis or reveal.
- **British spelling** throughout: characterise, recognise, analyse, signalling, favour, utilise. (Corpus contains one "favor" — treat as a slip, not licence.)
- **No serial comma.** "mitochondrial content, activity and central carbon fluxes".
- **Triples.** Lists of three nouns are strongly preferred over two or four.
- **Citations** in parentheses at the end of the clause or sentence they support, bundled where multiple.
- Passive voice is used freely and is not to be "corrected".
- Nominalisation is characteristic ("remodelling of ...", "identification of ...") and should not be verbalised away.

---

## **5. Connectives**

**In use.** However (sentence-initial, frequent; also mid-sentence after the subject: "Metabolic measurements themselves however are insufficient") · Moreover · Thus · Accordingly · Indeed · Crucially · Notably · Altogether · In addition · On the one hand / On the other hand · Alternatively · Given · In spite of · Whilst

**Absent, and to be avoided.** Asyndeton between independent clauses. Sentence fragments. "That said". "Here's the thing". Any connective doing rhetorical work rather than logical work.

First person plural is used freely and confidently: _we used · we show · we propose · we argued that · we have found · our findings reveal_.

## **7. Suppress these**

Default LLM habits that are not this voice. Observed in drafts during the 2026-09-17 session and corrected.

- Sentences of 4–10 words used for emphasis. **The commonest failure.**
- Em-dashes for parenthesis or reversal.
- One-sentence paragraphs.
- Colon used for dramatic reveal.
- Putting the claim into the "Here we ..." pivot instead of the sentence after it.
- Triadic fragments and asyndeton ("Not mass. Not ATP. Not membrane potential.").
- "not X but Y" _as a punch device_. The construction itself is permissible and occurs in the corpus, but only as plain syntax inside a longer sentence, never as the rhetorical shape of a short one.
- Stacked bold or headers inside continuous prose.

## **8. What does not transfer**

**Paragraph budget.** The Introduction of the MCbiclust methods paper spends ~250 words enumerating the limitations of existing tools. That is correct for a _Nucleic Acids Research_ methods paper and fatal in a length-capped Letter. Record the sentence architecture and connectives from paper 3; do **not** reproduce its expansiveness.

**Venue.** A Nature Metabolism Letter has no abstract and opens with a referenced introductory paragraph of ≤200 words, ~8 sentences. At the target sentence length in §1 that is all the room there is, so beats must be cut before drafting rather than trimmed afterwards.

## **11. Making this file active**

**Claude Code.** Reference it from the repo's `CLAUDE.md`, or wrap it as a skill so it triggers on manuscript-writing tasks rather than needing to be named.

**claude.ai (chat).** Two layers, and they do different jobs:

1. Upload this file to the **Project knowledge** of the relevant Project. It is then retrievable, but retrieval is not guaranteed on any given turn.
2. Add one scoping line to the **Project custom instructions**, which are loaded every turn: When drafting or revising manuscript prose in this Project, read STYLE.md from project knowledge and follow it. Do not apply it to conversational replies, analysis or explanation.

The second line is what makes the first reliable, and the scoping clause matters: this style is for manuscript text only.

**Do not put this in a custom Style.** A Style applies to everything Claude writes in the conversation, including analysis and discussion, which would make ordinary replies read like a journal Introduction. Use a Style only for a session that is pure drafting.

**Re-upload on revision.** Project knowledge holds a copy, not a live link to the repo. The repo remains the source of truth; bump the version in the header when they diverge.
